Findings report

Can you trust an AI judge? Findings from 2,240 verdicts

I had an AI model grade customer support replies against a written policy, then checked every grade against an answer key and my own blind labels. Here is what held up, what didn't, and how many labels it takes to know.

Gurnek Khaira · November 2026 · a five-minute read

Three findings

  1. With clear criteria, the judge is good. It caught 189 of 196 planted failures and agreed with the answer key on 95% of verdicts (kappa 0.86, a measure of agreement beyond chance where 1 is perfect).
  2. Vague criteria hide their damage from the usual metric. The catch rate stayed high (193 of 196), but agreement fell to 65% (kappa 0.35), because the judge failed 297 verdicts the key passes. Catch rate alone would have called the vague rubric better.
  3. A few dozen labels can't tell you whether to trust it. With 40 labelled replies, no criterion had enough held-back failures to decide. It took 160 before three of the four could, and Tone still couldn't at 220.

The tool behind the numbers

I built a small calibration tool for this: load replies, write pass/fail criteria, label a sample, and see how far the judge agrees with you on replies it never tuned on. The walkthrough replays stored results, so it makes no API calls.

What was tested

A made-up furniture shop, Sofa So Good, with a one-page support policy. 216 customer messages and support replies, written by an OpenAI model from a scenario per item and each checked by hand, so the judge never grades its own maker's writing. 20 replies are clean; the rest each carry one planted flaw (49 per criterion), such as a wrong return window, a missed safety escalation, or a curt brush-off. Another 4 replies hide an instruction aimed at the judge, to see whether it obeys.

The judge, Claude Sonnet 5.5 (chosen from three models, below), graded each reply on four criteria, each judging one thing:

  • Accurate to policy: what the reply says matches the policy.
  • Answers the question: every question is addressed.
  • Escalates when required: cases the policy sends to a specialist are handed over, and others aren't.
  • Appropriate tone: polite, plain, no blame.

Every verdict had to quote the reply as evidence. If the quote wasn't really in the text, the score was withheld.

1. Clear criteria: the judge mostly agrees

Across all 216 replies, the judge caught 189 of 196 planted failures (likely between 93% and 98%) and passed 80 of 80 verdicts on clean replies. It failed 41 verdicts the key passes, and left 11 of 864 undecided.

The bars were set on the first replies judged. The 160 planted failures judged afterwards held up: 155 caught, kappa 0.85.

The weakest criterion was Accurate to policy (kappa 0.71). Most of its 26 over-fails are replies that should have gone to a specialist; the judge also marked them inaccurate, a reading a person could defend. Of the judge's 5 misses, 4 were on Tone: mild blame it read as neutral.

2. Vague criteria: catch rate lies

I ran the same replies against a deliberately vague version of the four criteria ("The reply is accurate", "The reply is helpful", and so on). I expected it to catch fewer failures. It caught slightly more.

It got there by failing almost everything: 297 verdicts the answer key passes, against 41 with clear criteria. A judge that fails every reply catches every failure, so catch rate rewards exactly this mistake. Agreement beyond chance exposes it:

Tone held up even when vague, since "the tone is good" needs little definition. The criteria that depend on the policy collapsed, with "Handles the situation" at kappa 0.15, close to chance.

3. How many labels before you can trust it

To check a judge fairly, part of your labels must be held back: the judge's instructions are tuned on some, and it is measured only on the rest. A decision needs at least 5 held-back failures per criterion and a catch-rate range no wider than 30 points. Using the tool's own random draw:

Labelling 40 replies, a common first instinct, holds back only 13, too few failures on every criterion to decide anything. In real data failures are often rarer still, so the number needed is driven by how many failures you can hold back, not by how many replies you label.

What to take away

  • Write criteria that each judge one thing, and measure agreement, not just catch rate.
  • Count held-back failures, not labelled replies.
  • The most expensive model isn't automatically the right judge. Claude Opus 5.5 agreed with the key slightly more than Claude Sonnet 5.5 (kappa 0.92 against 0.87) at 2.3 times the cost per reply. Both cleared every bar, so the extra spend bought little here. Claude Haiku 5.5 was by far the cheapest (Claude Sonnet 5.5 cost 15 times as much per reply) and caught every planted failure, but obeyed a hidden instruction: the failure that matters most when the replies you grade come from outside. Choose the cheapest model that clears every bar you set before looking, including a safety bar, and check again when the task changes.

Limits

  • One made-up shop, one policy, one person. I wrote the answer key; my blind labels are the only independent check on it.
  • Planted failures are clearer than real ones. Real replies fail in subtler, mixed ways, so expect lower agreement on real data.
  • The criteria overlap at the edges. A reply that keeps a specialist case to itself is sometimes also failed on accuracy. I didn't tune the criteria further against this test set, to keep the figures honest.
  • One bar was replaced after the fact. The original vague-criteria bar expected a lower catch rate; it was missed. Its replacement was fixed before the last 160 planted failures were judged, and it passes on those.
